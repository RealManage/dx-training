Session 1 — Why CD & the Minimums

About how we deliver — not which platform we're on.

Set the frame. By the end they can: (1) explain why big batches are risky, (2) tell Continuous Delivery from Continuous Deployment, (3) recite the minimums and WHY each exists, (4) map our delivery value stream and find its biggest constraint. Tease the one question they'll hold all session — the litmus test on the second-to-last slide. Keep it warm: this is continuous improvement, not a verdict on anyone's stack.

Principles with deep roots

distills to five principles we live by

Small batches Fast feedback Built-in quality Deploy/release separation Continuous improvement
Almost nothing here was invented in software. The values come from Lean manufacturing — the Toyota Production System, refined on factory floors since the 1950s — and the postwar quality movement. Software relearned them the hard way and wrote them down as Continuous Delivery. The sell for the skeptic: CD isn't a fad — it's proven, cross-industry wisdom, validated for decades on factory floors before software ever adopted it. Three beliefs underneath: (1) Undeployed code is INVENTORY, not value — Ohno: inventory is waste; producing big batches to feel efficient is one of the most expensive habits on the floor. Code delivers nothing until it runs for a user. (2) Speed and safety rise TOGETHER, not a trade-off — DORA / Accelerate (2018): the teams that deploy most often also have the lowest change-failure rate. The mindset shift most teams resist. (3) You can't inspect quality in — you BUILD it in — Deming. A separate quality gate at the end is the thing to design out. At RealManage that's literal: no QA team, so the delivering team owns quality and the pipeline is where that becomes automated checks. Each principle traces to a root — small batches (Ohno; Reinertsen), fast feedback (Lean flow), built-in quality (Deming; Toyota jidoka), deploy/release separation (decouple technical risk from business risk — feature flags), continuous improvement (Kaizen; Goldratt's Theory of Constraints). Two factory images to use out loud: the ANDON CORD — any worker stops the line the moment a defect appears = our "stop the line on red"; and GENCHI GENBUTSU, "go and see" — observe the real work where it happens = the value-stream map you'll build today. When a practice seems arbitrary ("why short-lived branches?"), trace it back: practice → principle → belief. Small batches is first on purpose — that's the next two slides.

The big-batch trap

Deploying rarely manufactures risk.
A weekly release isn't one change — it's a WEEK of changes: diagnosis hell (which of 30 broke prod?), all-or-nothing rollback, release-day anxiety. Be fair to the weekly cadence — it gave a predictable window, a comms batching point, a forcing function for "is it ready?". CD must REPLACE each of those, not just delete them. The escape is counterintuitive: deploy MORE often, in SMALLER pieces.

Batch size is the master variable

Big batch → risk · Small batch → non-event conflicts · reviewability · blast radius · rollback
Walk 2–3 contrasts max: merge conflicts, reviewability, blast radius / isolation, rollback. If the room is AI-forward, land the AI angle: a human self-limits by friction — 1,200 lines is a day's grind; an agent emits them before lunch. So small batches become a THROTTLE you impose, not a habit you fall into. Evidence: DORA shows frequent small deploys give higher throughput AND stability (survey data — correlational, but strong and replicated year over year). Make it actionable: DORA's official Quick Check (dora.dev/quickcheck) benchmarks your four metrics against this year's industry cohorts in about two minutes — "where do we stand?" Re-run it after adopting these practices; the climb between runs is your improvement made visible.

CD ≠ Continuous Deployment

Delivery = a human decides when Deployment = auto on green
This confusion derails adoption. Continuous Delivery does NOT mean losing control — you GAIN the ability to deploy on your schedule instead of a weekly window, and releasing a feature to users stays a separate, deliberate call (that's what feature flags are for). Continuous Deployment — every green commit auto-to-prod — is a later, per-service choice once you trust the pipeline. You must reach Delivery first. We target Delivery.

Don't read 15 items aloud — let them land as "a floor we either meet or we don't." Puncture the big myth: "we have GitLab CI, so we do CI" — NO. A pipeline tool isn't the discipline; if branches live a week, you have a build server, not CI. These are the FLOOR, not stretch goals. Point them to the full reference for the list and the why.

The litmus test

Could a change committed now
reach prod today?

No re-typed commands. No readiness meeting.

This is the sentence that reframes the whole course. Most teams answer "no" — and the reasons WHY are their migration backlog, the rest of this course in miniature. Let it sit. Don't answer it for them. They'll carry this question through every session.

Now — map your value stream

Map idea → prod · process vs wait vs %C/A · name your #1 constraint Your turn →
Transition to the workshop — slides stop, hands-on starts. For every step capture three numbers: process time, wait time, %C/A (percent complete & accurate). Then derive lead time and flow efficiency. Most teams are shocked how low flow efficiency is — the worked example lands near 15%. The biggest wait is almost always the release window, not slow typing — confirm it from YOUR numbers. Homework: back the map with the current-state assessment (real numbers + minimums score), read the minimums reference, and bring one backlog feature for Session 2.
